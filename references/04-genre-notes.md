# Genre Notes

Corpus advice per genre. Read the one that applies. The loop is the same either way; what changes is how hard the corpus is to assemble and where the voice actually lives.

## Email

**The easy case.** High volume, real ground truth, clearly separable situations, and a natural range of readers in one corpus.

- Strip quoted reply chains before reading, or the audit will learn the other person's voice alongside theirs.
- Sent mail only. Drafts they never sent are not evidence of anything.
- The reader range is the asset here. One corpus can cover recruiters, close colleagues, and service providers, and the "how it changes by reader" section of the guide will be the strongest section.

## Cover letters

**The hard case, and the most commonly requested.**

Low volume, written under deadline, and the most template-contaminated writing most people own. Expect a corpus of four to eight, half of which were assembled from something they found online.

- Run it anyway. Mark the guide provisional.
- The audit will be thin. That is expected. The blind test does most of the work in this genre, not the audit.
- Expect the first guide to be mostly a ban list, because the genre pulls everyone toward the same corporate default and the useful early rules are all about what to stop doing.
- Do not substitute their email corpus. Their email voice is more relaxed and more confident than their cover letter voice, and a guide built from it will produce letters that read as too familiar for a stranger who is evaluating them.
- Worth telling them directly: their baseline here is probably the writing they like least, so the gap between "sounds like me" and "sounds like me on my best day" is widest in this genre. That is an argument for the loop, not against it.

## Text messages

**High volume, very short, and the voice lives somewhere unusual.**

- Message length and burst structure are part of the voice. Whether they send one long message or five short ones is a rule, and it is often the single most recognizable thing about how they text.
- So is punctuation: whether sentences end with periods, whether they double-space after them, whether they capitalize at all.
- Register splits hard by recipient, much harder than in email. The same person texts a recruiter and a sibling in what are effectively two different languages. Either split the corpus or make sure the "by reader" section carries real weight.
- Dictated messages contain transcription errors. Those are noise, not habits. Flag them and keep them out of the guide.

## Long-form posts and essays

**Low volume, high value per piece.**

- Structure is most of the voice at this length. How a piece opens, when it uses lists, how an argument gets introduced and how evidence is placed matter more than sentence-level habits.
- The corpus will be small, maybe five to ten pieces. Accept it.
- Test pairs are expensive here, since a brief plus a draft for a 1200 word essay is real work. Six to eight pairs is a reasonable target instead of the usual 10 to 15.
- Watch for co-authored pieces. Anything edited by a publication is not a clean sample of their voice, and anything an AI helped with is worse than useless.

## Internal memos and one-pagers

**Medium volume, and context-dependent in a way the others are not.**

- Jargon level and reader assumptions vary more by company than by person. If they changed jobs partway through the corpus, split it and pick one.
- Formal structure is often imposed by the organization rather than chosen. Separate the parts that are theirs from the parts that are the template, or the guide will encode their last employer's document standard as personal voice.
- The strongest rules in this genre tend to be about how they open an argument and how they handle uncertainty in front of an audience that can overrule them.

## A note on resumes

Not a genre for this method. A resume is evidence selection and bullet compression, and running this loop on one produces very little. If they ask, say so directly rather than letting them spend an afternoon finding out.
